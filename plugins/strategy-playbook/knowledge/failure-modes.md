# Failure Modes (Level 7)

Six ways the playbook fails, and the fix for each. Critics refer to these by number.

## 1. Unclear problem statement
The most common failure. The problem or goal isn't clear, so work goes slowly. Ask five people what it's about and you get five answers.
**Symptoms you hear:** everyone gives a different answer about what the initiative is for; the work is crawling or stalled; people keep asking why we are doing this; meetings relitigate the goal.
**Fix:** pressure-test it with people. Simplify until 9 out of 10 get it right.
**Signs in a doc:** no problem statement or motivation; a goal with no reader, date, or evidence; the reader test's first question has no answer.

## 2. Wrong tiger team
The team doesn't gel, is missing skill sets, or people don't have enough time.
**Symptoms you hear:** the team isn't gelling; nobody has time for it; we are missing a skill and keep waiting on other teams.
**Fix:** curate the team; work with managers to define the skill sets needed. Coach people, and if someone isn't coachable, substitute early rather than waiting for the project to fail.

## 3. Fake north star
It isn't really a north star. Either it's so vague that all work maps into it, or so specific it's a goal for a single project.
**Symptoms you hear:** every project says it maps to the vision; the vision doesn't help anyone decide what to drop; it reads like one project's goal.
**Fix:** test it with people. Can they use it to sort their work into "maps in" and "doesn't"?
**Signs in a doc:** "Be the best," "Deliver value to customers," anything every team already does; or a single milestone dressed as a vision.

## 4. Bets that aren't bets
The bet is so obvious no reasonable person would pick the other side. Then it's a roadmap: work with no real tradeoffs. Where real tradeoffs exist but aren't named, people optimize locally.
**Symptoms you hear:** nobody disagrees with any of it; it reads like a roadmap; teams still make opposite tradeoffs locally.
**Fix:** test it with people. Do some think the other side is right? If not, do they at least see the other side as a choice another company could reasonably make?
**Signs in a doc:** "Quality versus bugs," "Secure versus insecure," a bet with no "versus," or a principle that is really a contested choice.

## 5. Nobody knows it exists
A great strategy nobody has heard of. Local work goes on as usual, possibly executing the very tradeoffs you tried to steer away from, and the original problem gets worse.
**Symptoms you hear:** teams haven't heard of the strategy; local plans ignore it; the problem keeps getting worse anyway.
**Fix:** road show it, have champions share it out, and keep mapping the work into existing plans and OKRs.

## 6. Bad at feedback
Feedback is dismissed, not captured, or promised action never arrives. Dissent grows. People who claim to disagree and commit are paying lip service, because they don't see why they should trust you. The work moves very slowly.
**Symptoms you hear:** people say they agree but nothing changes; dissent keeps growing; feedback goes in and nothing comes out.
**Fix:** the level 6 practices: farm for disagreement, keep a feedback log, publish your feedback contract, and show what changed.

## Related doc-level defects the critics also flag
- **An outcome with no measure:** a key outcome that nobody could check as delivered. (Level 4: write outcomes so delivery is obvious; level 5: measures of success.)
- **Theory without data:** a claim of cost, value, or risk with no number, incident, or source. Executives expect data, value, and cost (level 6).
